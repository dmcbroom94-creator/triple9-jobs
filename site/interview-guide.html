Interview guide

How to prepare, what you are scored on, and how to answer, for service interviews and jobs outside the services.

How service interviews work

  • Most police, fire and ambulance interviews are structured and scored. Every candidate gets similar questions and is marked against a published framework.
  • You score points for evidence of behaviours, not for being likeable. Give specific examples, not “I would…” or “we usually…”.
  • Read the role profile and the framework before you prepare. Write two or three examples for each area so you are never stuck.
  • Interviewers often ask follow-up questions (“What would you do differently?”). Have a lesson learned ready for every example.

Police: what you are scored on

Most UK forces use the College of Policing Competency and Values Framework (CVF). Police Scotland and PSNI use their own versions of it.

Six competencies
We are emotionally awareWe take ownershipWe collaborateWe support and inspireWe analyse criticallyWe are innovative and open-minded
Three values (2024 CVF)
CourageRespect and empathyPublic service

Check the level your role is assessed at (1, 2 or 3). Sergeants and above need leadership examples.

Fire & rescue: what you are scored on

Fire and rescue services in England follow the national Core Code of Ethics, alongside each service’s own values and behaviours.

Five ethical principles
Putting our communities firstIntegrityDignity and respectLeadershipEquality, diversity and inclusion

Expect questions on teamwork, safety, community work and challenging poor behaviour.

Ambulance & NHS: what you are scored on

NHS trusts use values-based recruitment. Each trust has its own values, usually built on the six NHS Constitution values:

Working together for patientsRespect and dignityCommitment to quality of careCompassionImproving livesEveryone counts

Read your target trust’s values on its website and use their exact words in your answers.

Transferee & rejoiner interviews

  • “Why this force?” Read the Police and Crime Plan and the force’s priorities. Mention the area, its challenges and something specific you can bring.
  • “Why are you leaving your force?” Keep it positive: family, location, development, a specialism. Never criticise your current force.
  • Bring evidence: recent appraisals (PDRs), qualifications (e.g. PIP level, driving and public order courses) and your training record.
  • Vetting and references: be completely honest on forms. Unexplained gaps cause more problems than the issue itself.
  • Rejoiners: explain what you did while away and what new skills it gave you.

Common service interview questions

“Tell us about a time you dealt with a difficult or aggressive person.”Show calm communication first, then proportionate action. End with the outcome and what you learned.
“Describe a time you challenged inappropriate behaviour.”Interviewers want courage and integrity. Explain how you raised it, who you told, and the result.
“Tell us about a time you made a decision without all the information.”Walk through your thinking: risks, options, why you chose your action. The National Decision Model is a useful structure for police.
“How have you supported a colleague’s wellbeing?”Show emotional awareness: you noticed, you checked in, you signposted support.
“Tell us about a time you improved the way something was done.”Pick a real change with a measurable result, e.g. time saved or complaints reduced.

For every interview

Before the day

  • Read the job advert and highlight every skill they ask for.
  • Prepare one STAR example for each skill.
  • Research the organisation: website, news, values, annual report.
  • Practise out loud, ideally with a friend or by recording yourself.
  • Plan your route, or test your camera and microphone for video interviews.

On the day

  • Arrive 10 minutes early, or log in 5 minutes early online.
  • Take a notepad with your examples and questions.
  • Pause before answering. It looks thoughtful, not slow.
  • If you don’t understand a question, ask them to repeat it.
  • Nervous? Breathe in for four, hold for four, out for four before you go in.

After the interview

  • Write down the questions you were asked while you remember.
  • If unsuccessful, ask for feedback. It is often very specific.
  • Keep applying while you wait to hear back.
